How tides work

Twice a day the sea climbs up the beach and slides back. The reason is simple — and most people get one part of it wrong.

story

The moon pulls the ocean

The moon's gravity tugs Earth's water toward it, raising a bulge of ocean on the side facing the moon. Stand under that bulge and the sea rises: high tide.

story

Why two high tides?

Here's the twist: there's a second bulge on the far side of Earth, where the moon's pull is weakest and the water is effectively left behind. So most coasts get two high tides a day, not one.

story

The sun joins in

The sun tugs too. When sun and moon line up at new and full moon, the bulges add into big 'spring' tides. When they sit at right angles, they partly cancel into gentle 'neap' tides.

story

Why it's later each day

The moon is also orbiting while Earth spins, so it takes about 24 hours and 50 minutes to return overhead. That's why high tide slips roughly 50 minutes later every day.

end

That's tides

Two bulges, chasing the moon, nudged by the sun. Next time a tide table looks random, it's just the moon running about 50 minutes late.
